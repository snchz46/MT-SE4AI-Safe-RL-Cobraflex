# Chapter 11 — Discussion

## 11.1 Purpose and method

This chapter evaluates the framework, not the system. The distinction is the one established in §3.2.1 when the work was declared as constructive research: the artefact under judgement is the adapted V-Model, and the lane-following system is the instrument with which it has been put to the test. The method is the one fixed in advance in §3.7 — five criteria with their indicators — plus the evaluation of the three hypotheses of Chapter 1. Fixing the criteria before knowing the result is what allows this evaluation to be something more than a defence.

## 11.2 The framework against its five criteria

**(1) Traceability integrity — criterion: zero orphans. Met.** The validator passed with no orphans at every review gate and over the consolidated matrix at the close. The relevant fact is not the final zero but that the constraint operated as a hard gate during development: orphan identifiers were detected as they were introduced, not in a later audit. A zero obtained by a final audit would have measured the diligence of the author; obtained continuously, it measures a property of the framework.

**(2) Requirement coverage by evidence — criterion: 100 % with a verdict, even a negative one. Met.** The fourteen requirements have a verdict backed by quantitative evidence. The clause "an honest verdict is preferable to an omission" was really exercised, and in three directions: the global verdict of the camera arm was recorded as literally negative; two requirements keep their literal failure next to the reconciliation instead of being rewritten; and one requirement is closed as not satisfied, with its measurement and its breakdown by rule combination. The criterion was met not in spite of the uncomfortable results, but through them.

**(3) Hazard anticipation — criterion: most of what was observed had been anticipated and what emerges is auditable. Substantially met, and only partly interrogated by the physical step.** Almost all the hazards that appeared were in the register from the analysis phase, or were added when the camera track was opened, before the campaign measured them. Two cases are instructive because they emerged. The first one is the conflict between the cage and its own estimator: the envelope degrading a competent policy because of a confident but wrong reading; its root was registered as a hazard, but not its manifestation as a mode of its own. The second one is the degenerate optimum of stopping that appears when longitudinal authority is given to the policy: the register anticipated the family — reward exploitation — but the concrete instance had to be discovered. In both cases the framework absorbed the emergent case through the foreseen path: identifier, recorded decision, artefact.

A third emergent case deserves a separate mention because the framework did not anticipate it in any form: the dependency on a class B rule for lane keeping (§8.5). The hazard register considers that the cage may fail, may arbitrate badly or may read badly; it does not consider that the policy may adapt to the cage to the point of needing it in order to drive. It is a failure mode of the *coupling* between learned component and envelope, and it suggests a hazard category that the initial analysis did not have.

**The physical step submits this criterion to a harder test than simulation, and the result is qualified accordingly.** On hardware, the observed failures fall into two groups with different value for judging the criterion. The first one consists of manifestations inside families that were already registered but not described by them: the register considers that the cage estimator may read badly with confidence, but not that its accuracy may be a property of the place on the circuit — excellent at one point, with biases of tens of millimetres and no warning at others — which is how it manifested (§9.3.6). That the family was anticipated is what made it possible to absorb it; that its form was not is what forced it to be measured with a true-position capture before anything could be done about it. The second group is not about hazards at all, and there the criterion applies only by omission: rules that are correct against their specification but whose *operational behaviour* is not defined, and thresholds that cannot activate on commanded motion in the deployed configuration. A hazard register asks what can go wrong; it does not ask what happens afterwards, nor whether the operating point reaches the envelope that was specified.

The honest reading of the criterion is therefore met in simulation and partly interrogated on hardware: no physical manifestation required a new hazard family, but several required a new description, and the most uncomfortable class — the gap between specification and operation — falls outside the instrument. Chapter 9 quantifies it from the other side and with an asymmetry that should not be softened: the list of *gaps* written before touching hardware got right the terms that a simulator knows how to represent and contains none of the terms that actually stopped the vehicle.

**(4) Adoption cost — criterion: proportional to the benefit. Met provisionally.** The record contains more than seventy decisions, the validator, the templates and a dozen living documents kept up to date with the results by a single person *while* two training tracks, several evaluation campaigns and a bridge between simulators were being executed. The exact split between framework effort and technical effort was not measured in hours — a limit declared in advance; the recorded indicator is that no review gate was delayed by framework artefacts, while there were indeed delays for technical reasons.

**(5) Productivity of the matrix — criterion: documented cases where it accelerated the impact analysis. Met, with a counterexample.** Three concrete cases: the criterion hygiene defect that produced the literal negative verdict was localised in minutes by following the row requirement → scenario → clause, because the chain was materialised; the re-scoring of a requirement on its own metric was possible because the matrix distinguished which scenario exercises which requirement with which metric; and the migration of the library to a new geometry reused the existing chains with auditable changes.

The counterexample is recorded with the same weight, because it points to a real limit of the framework: the matrix did not detect on its own that a scenario was being executed without injecting its initial conditions, so that for a while it produced zero co-activation and an indeterminate verdict that looked like an instrumentation gap. Traceability guarantees that the scenario exists and references its requirement; it does not guarantee that the executor runs it as specified. That distinction — validation of *execution* against validation of *reference* — is the most concrete improvement that this work identifies over its own framework.

## 11.3 The three hypotheses

**H1 (construct) — supported.** The five adaptations were enough to cover the failure modes found along the cycle, and none of them required inventing a mechanism outside the framework. The qualification is that "were enough" means "none was found that did not fit", which is evidence of practical sufficiency and not of completeness.

**H2 (operability) — supported.** Each adaptation produced concrete artefacts, maintained by one person, without any of them becoming the bottleneck of the project. The cheapest adaptation turned out to be the traceability one — a validator of a few hundred lines — and it is also the one that produced the most value, which is the clearest cost-benefit result of the work.

**H3 (usefulness) — supported with an important qualification.** The framework produced a well-founded verdict with its validity limits, which is exactly what the hypothesis claimed. The qualification is that the verdict is uncomfortable: globally negative in its literal form, with one unsatisfied requirement and one rule that was never exercised. A reader might read this as a failure of the system; that would be a level error. The hypothesis is about the ability of the framework to produce a traceable judgement, not about the sign of that judgement, and a framework that could only produce favourable judgements would not be useful but decorative.

## 11.4 Lessons learned

**Traceability is cheap and instrumentation is expensive.** The validator cost little and returned a lot; what consumed time was making the scenarios really measure what they said they measured — injecting initial conditions, logging the quantities that the criteria reference, distinguishing indeterminacy from failure. The lesson for anyone replicating the framework is that the effort has to be budgeted for the instrumentation, not for the documentation.

**An inherited criterion that is not audited contaminates the verdict.** A performance clause calibrated on an earlier controller and an earlier geometry survived two migrations and blocked the global verdict of two campaigns. Auditing it revealed that it measured a property different from the one its name suggested. The lesson: acceptance criteria are version-controlled artefacts and must be migrated with the same discipline as the code.

**Training metrics do not classify behaviour.** Seeds with indistinguishable curves produced policies that fall on opposite sides of the line separating "respects the constraints" from "depends on the envelope". A framework that accepted selection by reward would have delivered, in this work, the worst of three candidate policies.

**A measurement taken at one point does not characterise an operating envelope.** This is the lesson that the physical step imposed most insistently and the easiest one to repeat for whoever comes next. The camera calibration, the check of the lateral error against a tape measure and all the pre-deployment verifications were taken with the vehicle stopped at the same place on the circuit, for the practical reason that it was the convenient place; a capture over the complete layout revealed that this point was the best one on the circuit for the estimator, and with it that three single-pose conclusions did not generalise. The content of those measurements stood; what was withdrawn was their scope. The lesson is not that they were badly taken, but that the protocol of a measurement must cover the domain over which something is going to be claimed, and that an acceptance gate based on dispersion cannot detect a stable bias.

**The envelope can shape the component it contains.** This is the most unexpected lesson and the one with the widest consequences: when training with the cage in the actuation chain, what is optimised is the pair, not the policy. Runtime containment is not a neutral filter over a given component; it is part of the system that the component learns to inhabit.

## 11.5 Comparison with alternative practices

Against not using a framework, the observable difference is that claims have a destination: every result has exactly one place to be recorded and none is left without evidence. Against model-based engineering, the framework gives up the power of a central model in exchange for a much lower entry barrier — version-controlled text files and a validator — while keeping the essential property of verified traceability. Against pure safe RL, it keeps a guarantee that does not depend on the training distribution and that survives the replacement of the policy. Against scenario-based validation without a life cycle, it adds the upward connection: every scenario exists because a requirement asks for it and a hazard justifies it, instead of because it looked interesting.

## 11.6 Residual risks

Five risks remain open at the close and are stated without mitigation. The arbitration under co-activation does not meet its requirement and its correction is an open design problem. The dependency on the rate limiter means that what transfers to hardware is a coupled pair and not a policy. The common cause between the perception of the policy and the perception of the cage is structural: they share the image, and a sufficiently severe degradation blinds both at once; the existing mitigations degrade to a safe stop but do not remove the dependency. Physical evidence exists but it is not scored: the chain has been executed on the real vehicle and it drives, but no run has been executed under the scenario protocol, so that no verdict claim in this work is validated on the real platform. And the fifth one is the one that most bounds what the framework can claim: the envelope has never acted on the real vehicle. All the physical runs were in monitoring, with the safe action identical to the raw action in all the logged cycles, so that the central artefact of this work — a cage that corrects at runtime — has been verified in simulation and only *observed* on hardware. The framework produced the evidence that allows this to be said precisely, which is its function; it does not produce, and does not claim to produce, the evidence that would be needed to say the opposite.

## 11.7 Synthesis

The framework meets the five criteria it set for itself, including the one that demanded verdicts for all the requirements even if they turned out to be unfavourable. Its most instructive limit is none of those declared in advance but the one it discovered when it was applied: traceability validates references, not executions, and that asymmetry allowed one scenario to run for months without doing what its specification said. Chapter 12 collects the conclusions and translates these findings into concrete lines of work.
