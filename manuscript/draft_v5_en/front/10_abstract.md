# Abstract

The functional safety frameworks of the automotive industry, led by the ISO 26262 V-Model, assume that a component's behaviour can be derived from a specification written in advance, checked against expected outputs and validated before deployment. Components trained with reinforcement learning (RL) break all three assumptions. Their behaviour comes out of a stochastic optimisation, there is no "correct answer" to compare their output with, and their robustness outside the training data can only be measured empirically.

This thesis proposes and evaluates an adapted V-Model with five explicit changes (A1–A5). These changes bring an RL component into a traceable development cycle that is consistent with ISO 26262, ISO 21448 (SOTIF), ISO/IEC TR 5469 and UL 4600, without losing the two-way link between specification and V&V that makes the standard valuable. The framework is applied to a lane-following case study with a 1:14 scale vehicle (CobraFlex) trained in the Gazebo simulator. The policy is end-to-end from the front camera: a convolutional network that learns perception as well as control. It is contained by a safety cage of six deterministic rules that works on its own vision-based lane estimator, independent of the network. A second version, using a privileged state vector, acts as a control arm to isolate the effect of the cage and to measure the cost of perception.

The main contribution is a method: an executable life cycle whose two-way traceability is checked by software along the chain Hazard → Safety Requirement → Cage Rule → Scenario → Metric → Logged Evidence → Verdict, so that no gate review can be closed with orphan identifiers on either side.

The reference validation campaign runs 27 scenarios × 2 modes = 1,890 runs on the `complex_b` circuit. The modes are *enforcement* (the cage acts) and *monitoring* (the cage observes but does not act), and the policy has a two-dimensional action (steering and throttle) and was chosen for how it drives, not for its reward. The results support three claims. First: inside the operational domain there is not a single contact with the road edge with the cage active, against 60 made by the policy alone when the cage only observes. Outside the domain, the number drops to less than half of the previous version. Second: inside the domain, the cage's safety rules are latent (C-01/C-02/C-03/C-05 do not fire in clean nominal driving), and the cage becomes the active mechanism exactly where perception degrades or the vehicle leaves the domain. Third, and less comfortable: the rate limiter C-06, on paper a non-critical smoothness rule, turns out to be what keeps the vehicle in the lane on the tightest curves. Without the cage, the same policy leaves the road in 17 out of 25 endurance runs. The dependency is measured, and its origin (co-adaptation to the limiter during training) is stated as inferred. This transfer risk was stated before touching any hardware, and the first measurement on the real vehicle does not confirm it: with the checkpoint chosen for independence from the cage rather than for reward, the limiter acts in 3.4 % of the cycles on hardware against 3.0 % in simulation. This is one run, so the question is not settled.

The work also reports a negative verdict. The rule composition requirement (SR-010) is not met: when the lateral and heading rules fire at the same time, violations of the lateral margin still happen. Training a better policy cuts the problem in half without changing its nature, which makes it a design problem of the cage and not of the policy. It is stated as a limitation and as future work instead of being explained away. Finally, the sim-to-real gap is measured in steps of increasing realism (Gazebo → Isaac Sim → physical platform). The physical step was carried out as a bring-up and closed without a results campaign. Even so, the bring-up already gives five things that simulation could not, and four of them do not depend on the campaign. The first is an inherited assumption that turned out to be false: the camera's effective field of view is 77.89°, not the 90° that the simulator copied. This error affects every lateral quantity the cage uses, and no amount of extra simulation would have revealed it, because the simulator had inherited it. The second is a negative result: the policy validated in simulation does not transfer, because it memorised the 6.5:1 handedness of the training circuit as a steering bias. The third is a preliminary observation (one run): a policy retrained for transfer drives 18.05 m of the real circuit in one segment without triggering any safety rule. The fourth is a failure mode that simulation cannot show at all: the lane estimator fails depending on the place on the circuit and not on what the vehicle is doing, measured with true-position methods. The fifth is a type of defect that only appears with a real vehicle in front of you: rules that are correct by specification but have no defined behaviour in operation, and sensors whose failures go straight into the cage's only speed input. No scenario has been scored on hardware and the cage has never changed an action on the real vehicle. The physical evidence is therefore bring-up evidence and not verdict evidence: the physical campaign is stated as not executed and left as future work, and the gap table keeps its physical column as provisional.

The result is a case study that is traceable from start to finish and shows the feasibility, the adoption cost and the limits of the proposed framework.
