# Chapter 12 — Conclusions and future work

## 12.1 Purpose

This chapter closes the work in three movements: it synthesises the findings that the thesis supports with evidence, it answers the research questions posed in Chapter 1, and it translates the open residuals into concrete lines of work with a success criterion. It is ordered by **demonstrated value**, not chronologically, and the negative findings occupy as much space as the positive ones because they have equal status as a result.

## 12.2 Synthesis of results

The results are labelled `R1…R14` and are cited by that identifier. The convention avoids a
collision with the two namespaces that the document already uses: `H-01…H-12` are the **hazards**
of the register (Chapter 4 and Appendix A) and `H1…H3` are the **hypotheses** stated in §1.4 and
evaluated in §11.3.

**R1 — The complete traceability chain can be operationalised by a single person.** The chain `Hazard → Requirement → Rule → Scenario → Metric → Evidence → Verdict` was kept tool-verified throughout the whole project, with no orphans at any review gate, with a validator of marginal cost. It is the central claim of the thesis and the one with the best evidence.

**R2 — The literal verdict and the own criterion can diverge, and the divergence is informative.** The global verdict of the reference campaign is literally negative, attributable in its entirety to one performance clause on a single scenario, while the safety predicates are fully met. Keeping both readings in the record — the literal failure and the argued reconciliation — is more honest than rewriting the verdict, and it exposes a real defect of criterion hygiene that a retroactive edit would have hidden.

**R3 — The aggregation semantics matter: indeterminate is not failure.** A per-run verdict that references a quantity that was not logged is an instrumentation gap, not a violation. Distinguishing them is what allowed the resulting measurements, once the gaps were closed, to be interpretable as results — one positive, one negative — and not as artefacts of the aggregator.

**R4 — The training curve does not classify the behaviour.** Seeds with indistinguishable reward curves produce policies that fall on opposite sides of the boundary between respecting the constraints and depending on the envelope. The operational consequence is that the selection has to be made by closed-loop evaluation with the intervention rate as a first-order criterion.

**R5 — The checkpoint at the reward peak can be the worst candidate.** In the reference policy it was: fourteen safety interventions against zero in the chosen candidate. It is the practical corollary of the previous finding and it also works as a control against the objection of selection after the fact, because the criterion was fixed before the campaign.

**R6 — The cage is latent when the policy respects the constraints, and its value is measured where perception degrades.** With perfect perception the envelope does not intervene inside the domain; under a degraded camera it **removes failures that the policy commits on its own**. The contrast between the two arms is what turns the claim "the cage adds safety" into a measured claim and not into an architectural assumption.

**R7 — The safety invariant holds: zero edge contacts inside the domain with the cage active**, against sixty that the policy commits without it, over 945 enforcement runs.

**R8 — End-to-end learning from the camera beats the classical baseline in tracking precision** on winding geometry, reversing the result obtained on simple geometry. It is the finding that justifies the cost of the learned component in this domain.

**R9 — Rule composition is a real problem, not a theoretical one.** Under simultaneous co-activation of the lateral and heading rules, violations of the lateral margin persist. Training better **halves the finding without changing its nature**, which places it as a design problem of the envelope. It is the only requirement that the work closes as not satisfied, and it is reported as such.

**R10 — Fail-safe conservatism costs availability, and that cost is measurable.** The controlled stops that avoid excursions also interrupt runs that would have finished well. The design of the trigger is an explicit trade-off between safety and availability, not a free choice.

**R11 — The gap between simulators is itself a result.** Porting the system to an environment of higher fidelity required recalibrating the dynamic authority, the heading thresholds and the structure of the reward itself. **Checkpoints do not transfer.** That negative result calibrated the expectation about the jump to hardware better than any optimistic estimate, **and the hardware confirmed it**: the policy validated in simulation keeps the sign of the response to the lane but with an amplitude one order of magnitude too small against a constant bias 2.1 times larger than the whole excursion, and the cause turned out to be the handedness of the training circuit memorised as a steering bias. Driving required a transfer-oriented retraining, not an adjustment.

**R12 — The cage does not only filter the policy: it shapes it.** Trained with the envelope in the actuation chain, the reference policy emits an almost all-or-nothing command that saturates the rate limiter in 77.5 % of the steps; with the cage active it drives better than any other configuration in this work, and without it, it leaves the lane roughly every three laps. What has been built — and what was transferred to hardware — is **the pair**, not the policy. The dependency is measured; its origin is inferred. **And the transfer of the pair has been measured once:** by choosing the checkpoint for independence from the cage instead of by reward, the limiter intervenes in **3.4 % of the cycles on the real vehicle against 3.0 % in simulation**. The risk was declared before measuring and the first measurement does not confirm it; with a single run and no scored scenario that does not close it, and the physical campaign is what decides.

**R13 — An assumption that the simulation inherits is an assumption that the simulation cannot falsify.** The effective field of view of the camera was a configuration default value that the simulator replicated, and it scaled every lateral quantity on which the cage acts. The first measurement on hardware refuted it — 77.89° against the 90° assumed — and on the unrectified image the lane boundary threshold, fixed at 160 mm, could trigger with the vehicle at a real 207–241 mm. Rectifying the image towards the canonical model removed the defect: when the measurement was repeated on the deployed path, the scale is 1.058/0.991 with no intercept and the threshold triggers at a real 151–158 mm. **The correction of that second figure belongs to the finding and does not weaken it**: neither the inherited error nor the effectiveness of its correction was observable in simulation, because the validation circuit was closed on itself. It is the strongest argument in the work in favour of making the physical step mandatory and not optional.

**R14 — There is a class of defect that only appears with a vehicle in front of you.** Rules that are correct in their specification but whose *operational* behaviour is not defined — an emergency stop whose asymmetric exit is correct for a simulated episode, which ends, and which leaves a vehicle that has to keep operating stopped indefinitely; thresholds that cannot activate in the deployed configuration; and sensor failure modes that enter directly into the only speed input of the cage. None of these is a failure of the policy or of the cage: they are gaps between an artefact validated against episodes and a vehicle that operates continuously.

## 12.3 Answer to the research questions

**Main question.** *Is it possible to adapt the canonical V-Model through a finite and traceable set of modifications that accommodates components trained by reinforcement learning without abandoning the bidirectional specification↔V&V correspondence?* **Yes, with the evidence of one case.** Five adaptations were enough to cover all the failure modes encountered; none of them required abandoning the structure of the V; the bidirectional correspondence was not only preserved but **hardened**, moving from a recommendation to a tool-verified constraint. The limit of the answer is that of a single case: practical sufficiency has been demonstrated, not completeness.

**Subordinate question.** *Does the framework produce coherent and traceable evidence, including an honest characterisation of the sim-to-real gap?* **Yes as far as traceable evidence is concerned; partially as far as the gap is concerned.** The fourteen requirements have a supported verdict, including the unfavourable ones. The characterisation of the gap is structured, **its first step measured and its second step brought up**: the high-fidelity bridge produced a useful negative diagnosis, and the physical platform has produced — in the bring-up phase — a falsified assumption, a negative result about the validated policy, a favourable preliminary observation about its replacement, the first measurement of a transfer risk declared in advance, and the localisation, with true-position metrology, that **the accuracy of the lane estimator is a property of the place on the circuit and not of the action of the vehicle** — a failure mode that no campaign in simulation could exhibit, because in simulation the estimator has no place to be in. What has **not** been produced is the physical campaign and therefore a per-scenario verdict on hardware: the measurement exists as bring-up and not as verdict, the physical column of the consolidated table is still empty for that reason, and it is declared as such. The part of A5 that remains pending is therefore narrower than it was and is stated precisely instead of as an absence, **but it is still pending**.

## 12.4 Future work

**T1 — Design and verification of the arbitration between rules.** It is the residual that no policy improvement solved and the only unsatisfied requirement. The direction is to move from composition by fixed order to an analysis of the joint envelope: to characterise in which subregion of the state space the sequential composition produces actions outside the envelope that each rule guarantees separately, and either to reorder and reparameterise, or to document that subregion as an exclusion from the domain. The breakdown by rule combination already indicates where to look — the co-activation of lateral limit and heading — and the existing scenario grid is the measuring instrument.

**T2 — Score scenarios on hardware.** The physical deployment is done and the vehicle drives; what is missing is the half that produces a verdict. Four conditions separate it from being executable, and all four are identified — one of them measured after the vehicle drove for the first time: **widen the lane estimator**, whose accuracy depends on the place on the circuit and not on the action, before restricting the policy. The remaining three: a reset path for the emergency stop, so that a scored run does not end at the first perception pulse — resolved outside the cage, precisely so as not to modify the artefact under verification; per-run provenance logging, already implemented; and a decision about which perception contract to use, because the heading setting with which the vehicle **can** drive is not the same one under which the simulation campaigns were scored. The prediction that this work made about which component would transfer best — the cage itself, because it is specified over an abstract state — **was fulfilled**: no safety rule activated during the run, and the only one that acted did so at the rate that simulation predicted.

**T3 — Ablation of the rate limiter.** The measurement of result R12 is done; its mechanism is still inferred. Retraining with the limiter out of the actuation loop and comparing the distribution of the raw command would close the question, and it is cheap in simulation. The original recommendation was that it should precede the hardware; the hardware arrived first and **does not invalidate it**: the fact that the pair transfers at the expected rate says that the dependency survives the change of plant, not where it comes from.

**T4 — Temporal robustness of the cage estimator.** There is no robust single-frame correction for a wrong but self-consistent reading. The well-founded directions are multi-frame consistency — a stable false reading in the same section of the circuit would be detectable as an inconsistency with the odometry — calibration of the confidence of the estimator against the simulation oracle, and cheap sensor redundancy for the cage state.

**T5 — Availability: robustness of the emergency trigger.** To dimension hysteresis and persistence in the validity and perception health triggers so that spurious stops do not occur, without lengthening the reaction time to genuine degradation. The acceptance criterion is already measurable over the existing library.

**T6 — Validation of execution, not only of reference.** This is the improvement that the framework identified in itself. Extending the validator to check that a scenario **is executed as specified** — that its initial conditions are injected, that the quantities its criterion references are logged — would close the gap that allowed one scenario to run for months without doing what it said it did.

**T7 — Replication and scale.** To replicate the framework on a second case with a learned component and on a photorealistic simulator, in order to separate what is a property of the framework from what is a property of this case and of this instrument.

## 12.5 Conclusion

The thesis argues that a classical functional safety life cycle can accommodate a component trained by reinforcement learning without giving up what gives it value — the verifiable correspondence between what is specified and what is verified — and it argues this with an end-to-end executed case whose evidence chain is auditable in its entirety.

The most solid result is also the simplest one: inside the declared operational domain, with the envelope active, the system did not leave the road a single time in nine hundred and forty-five runs, while without it, it did so sixty times. The most instructive result, however, is uncomfortable: the envelope that makes that figure possible **also shaped the policy it contains**, to the point that the policy is no longer viable without it. And the result that the work hands over as an open problem is the only requirement it does not satisfy: the arbitration between simultaneous rules, measured over two different policies and persistent in both.

There is a fourth result that does not come from simulation and that the work would not have obtained without it either. The physical step did not serve to confirm the simulated results — it did not score a single scenario, and this is declared — but to **falsify assumptions that the simulation could not reach**: a camera parameter that the simulator had inherited and therefore replicated, the handedness of a training circuit learned as a steering bias, and an estimator whose accuracy depends on where the vehicle is and not on what it does. No additional amount of simulation would have revealed any of the three, because in all three cases the instrument shared the error with the object measured. That is the strongest argument this thesis can offer in favour of making the characterisation of the gap a mandatory component of the cycle and not an optional appendix, and it is also what explains why adaptation A5 is stated as a requirement and not as a recommendation.

That the four coexist in the same document, with the same traceability and without any of them having been rewritten to look more presentable, is the best available evidence that the framework does what it says it does.
