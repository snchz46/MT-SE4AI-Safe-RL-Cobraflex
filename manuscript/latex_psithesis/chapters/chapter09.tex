\chapter{Characterisation of the Sim-to-Real Gap}
\label{ch:09}

\section{The gap as an object of study}
\label{sec:9.1}

Adaptation A5 requires operational validation to incorporate, as a mandatory and not optional component, the explicit characterisation of the gap between the environment where the system was trained and the one where it is going to operate. The requirement responds to an asymmetry in the literature noted in \S\ref{sec:2.7}: techniques to \emph{reduce} the gap are abundant, and frameworks to \emph{measure} it in terms usable by a safety argument are scarce.

This chapter treats the gap as an object of study in \textbf{steps of increasing fidelity}: the simulator where all the verdict evidence lives; a bridge of higher physical and visual fidelity; and the real platform. Each step answers a different question, and the chapter declares precisely which of them has a measured answer today and which does not.

\section{First step: the high-fidelity bridge}
\label{sec:9.2}

An equivalent environment was built on a simulator with a physics engine and rendering of higher fidelity, with the complete chain ported: import of the vehicle model, sensor publication, in-process training, domain randomization and the same cage with its parameter file.

The most useful result of this step \textbf{is not a campaign but a diagnosis}, and it deserves to be reported as such because it is exactly the kind of information that A5 aims to obtain. Porting the system revealed a series of obstacles that were not of implementation but of \textbf{coupling between layers}: the yaw authority of the model needed recalibration because the dynamic response differs; the heading thresholds of the cage had to be recalibrated for the new renderer because the vision estimator reads the same lane with a different bias; and the policy with longitudinal authority discovered a degenerate optimum --- stopping --- that the earlier environment did not allow it to express.

The central lesson of the step follows from those obstacles: \textbf{checkpoints do not transfer between simulators}. A policy trained in one is not a valid policy in the other, not even with the same architecture and the same reward; each environment requires retraining. If that happens between two simulators, the reasonable expectation about the jump to the physical platform should be calibrated accordingly. It is a negative result, and it is informative.

\section{Second step: the physical platform}
\label{sec:9.3}

\subsection{Status}
\label{sec:9.3.1}

\begin{quote}
	\textbf{What follows is the \emph{bring-up} of the physical step, not its results campaign}, and the distinction should be read before the figures. The physical evidence in this chapter is of two classes with different evidential value. \textbf{Calibration measurements and structural findings} --- a metrology against a reference standard, a regression over 5,665 cycles, two controlled pairs, a threshold comparison and a true-position capture over the complete layout --- which are results and do not depend on any campaign. And \textbf{driving figures}, which come from runs executed \textbf{outside the scenario protocol} and are therefore \textbf{preliminary}: those of nominal tracking, from a single run (\S\ref{sec:9.3.4}); those of envelope blocking, from three (\S\ref{sec:9.3.7}). The physical campaign would replace the second class and not the first.
\end{quote}

The physical deployment chain is built, documented end to end and \textbf{executed on hardware}: embedded camera, policy node, lane estimator, cage node, vehicle control and platform driver, running on the real vehicle on a closed lane circuit. The step has produced evidence, and that evidence is of three classes that should not be mixed: \textbf{calibration measurements} on the hardware, a \textbf{negative result} about the policy that simulation had validated, and a \textbf{positive result} about the policy retrained to transfer.

What it has \textbf{not} produced is a scored scenario, and the three reasons are independent of each other: the driving was done outside the protocol of the library, in \mon\ and not in \enf, and with a perception setting different from the one that scored the campaigns. Any one of the three is enough for nothing in this chapter to be a verdict; the three of them apply together. \Cref{ch:10} therefore declares its physical column \emph{not executed} (\S10.4d--e), and everything that follows here is posterior evidence. That column should not be confused with the one in the \textbf{gap table} of \S\ref{sec:9.4}, which does carry physical figures: the first one scores requirements under protocol and is empty of content by decision; the second one compares driving quantities outside the protocol and is preliminary by construction.

\subsection{The blocking check was executed, and it failed}
\label{sec:9.3.2}

The element identified as blocking was the effective field of view of the embedded camera: a configuration default value that the simulation had \textbf{replicated}, so that no simulation result could expose an error in it, and that scales every lateral quantity on which the cage acts. It was measured before any driving metric, as the chapter had announced, and \textbf{the value was wrong}: the effective horizontal field of view of the real module is \textbf{\qdeg{77.89}}, not the \qdeg{90} assumed.

The consequence was measured afterwards against a tape measure, with the vehicle displaced by hand over a range of $\pm$100 mm, and \textbf{on the unrectified image}: the estimator then read the lateral error as approximately \textbf{0.68--0.83 times the real value minus 10 mm}, an under-reading that resisted all filtering and that would have triggered the boundary threshold\sidenote{Fixed at 160 mm.} with the vehicle at a real 207--241 mm. The lane width, on the other hand, was read correctly --- 252.9 mm against a 250 mm ruler --- because a width is a \emph{difference} that straddles the optical axis while the lateral error is an \emph{absolute position} off it, and the unmodelled distortion term compresses only the second one. That diagnosis is what decided the correction, and its prescription was explicit: \textbf{rectify, do not reparameterise}.

\begin{quote}
	\textbf{Partial retraction, and in favour of the correction adopted.} The figures in the previous paragraph characterise the \emph{unrectified path} and \textbf{do not describe the deployed system}. When the measurement was repeated on the rectified image --- nine points, vehicle on the ground, untouched --- the scale is \textbf{1.058 on the left and 0.991 on the right}, and the intercept disappears: the 160 mm threshold triggers with the vehicle at a real \textbf{151--158 mm}, with about 100 mm of margin to the edge and not between 14 and 48. The under-reading was real, it was a defect of the optical path and not of the estimator, and rectification removed it. The original number is kept because it is the evidence that justified the decision; \textbf{no cage rule should be tuned from it}.
\end{quote}

This is exactly the kind of finding that adaptation A5 exists to produce, and it deserves to be stated as such: \textbf{an assumption that simulation could not falsify, because simulation had inherited it, turned out to be wrong on the first measurement on hardware, and it was in the path of a safety rule.} The correction adopted is not to reparameterise the estimator but to \textbf{rectify the real image towards the canonical model} --- so that a single projection serves both the simulation campaigns and the vehicle --- and its effect is measured on hardware with the car stopped and untouched: the cycles with invalid perception fall from \textbf{45\,\% to 5.5\,\%}, and the lane boundary rule goes from triggering \textbf{102 times without the car moving to not triggering at all}. The nine-point measurement cited above confirms it in the magnitude that matters.

\subsection{The policy validated in simulation does not transfer}
\label{sec:9.3.3}

The first real drive with the reference policy --- the two-dimensional one that the verdict campaign had evaluated --- produced a clean negative result. With the vehicle displaced by hand over a range of 332 mm of lateral error and the chain running without actuation, over 5,665 logged cycles, the steering command responds to the position \textbf{with the correct sign but with an amplitude one order of magnitude too small}: the lane-dependent excursion over the complete 332 mm is 0.055, against a \textbf{constant bias towards the left of 0.1155}, that is 2.1 times the whole excursion; and only \textbf{29 of the 5,665 samples (0.5\,\%) command a right turn}. In closed loop the bias dominates and the vehicle leaves the lane towards the left regardless of where it is.

The diagnosis of the bias is the training circuit itself. Driven clockwise, it presents approximately \textbf{13 m of left arc against 2 m of right arc per lap}, an asymmetry of 6.5 to 1, and the action log of the training keeps the mean steering at $+0.112\ldots+0.120$ \textbf{flat over its 284,672 steps}. It is not a perception defect or a dynamics defect: it is \textbf{the handedness of the track memorised as a steering bias}, a property of the training distribution that no appearance randomization would have corrected.

\subsection{The transfer-oriented retraining, and its result}
\label{sec:9.3.4}

The gap was decomposed into three separable terms, of which only the first one is a property of the track: \textbf{handedness} (observation and action mirrored per episode), \textbf{photometry} (three quarters of the episodes in the illumination band measured in the hall) and \textbf{camera geometry} (mount pitch, height and, in one tenth of the episodes, the real measured lens). A new policy was trained on that basis for 2.5 million steps.

The deployed checkpoint \textbf{is not the one with the maximum reward}. It was chosen by two criteria --- quality of the response under the deployment conditions and \textbf{independence from the cage} --- and the chosen candidate triggers the rate limiter in \textbf{3.0\,\%} of the nominal cycles against \textbf{35.0\,\%} for the reward peak. The second criterion is deliberate: the transfer risk declared in \Cref{ch:08} was precisely the coupling of the policy to the limiter, so that a checkpoint that leans on it is the wrong candidate to take to hardware. It is the second time in this work that the reward curve chooses badly.

The handedness term was closed: the bias/excursion ratio falls to \textbf{0.07--1.10} against \textbf{12.9--19.2} for the reference policy as it was deployed.

\textbf{And it transfers --- first measurement, one single run.} The retrained policy covered \textbf{18.05 m of the real circuit in a single uninterrupted segment of 101.1 seconds}, without operator intervention, keeping a median lateral error of \textbf{18.7 mm} (maximum 98.7 mm, against a boundary threshold of 160) and a maximum heading error of \textbf{\qdeg{18.91}} against a limit of 25. \textbf{No safety rule was activated during the run}; the only rule that acted was the rate limiter, in \textbf{3.4\,\% of the moving cycles against the 3.0\,\% measured in simulation}.

That last pair of figures is the most important observation of the bring-up, because \textbf{it is the first measurement of a question that \Cref{ch:08} had left open}: the transfer risk identified in advance was that the physical actuator does not implement the per-cycle variation limit on which the policy had leaned, and therefore that the pair \emph{(policy, limiter)} might not survive the hardware. In the first run it survived, with the two figures separated by four tenths of a percentage point. \textbf{With a single run this does not refute the risk} --- the campaign may contradict it --- but the distance is not marginal and the direction is the opposite of the one feared. That the risk was declared before touching hardware and that the first measurement interrogates it is what the framework asked of the physical step; closing it requires the campaign.

\subsection{What stops the vehicle is not the policy}
\label{sec:9.3.5}

The run ended \textbf{2.11 m short of completing the circuit}, and the cause is not one of driving. A single \textbf{400 ms} pulse of invalid perception --- one from which the estimator recovered by itself --- latched the emergency stop with the vehicle \textbf{27 mm from the lane centre}, on the tightest curve of the layout, and the asymmetric exit of that rule kept it latched for the remaining 396 s. The rule did exactly what was specified: its exit requires an explicit reset, a decision informed by the hazard analysis in order to avoid oscillation at the boundary of the trigger. \textbf{The gap is not in the cage but between an artefact validated against simulated episodes, which end, and a vehicle that has to keep operating.} It has been resolved by leaving the rule intact and placing the reset path outside the cage, precisely so as not to modify the artefact under verification with a change whose entire effect is on hardware and which simulation could not validate --- because there the latch is nearly inert.

Two more findings of the same class, both unrelated to the policy. The visual odometry of the stereo camera, when it closes a loop, reinitialises its pose and \textbf{jumps}; since the state filter fuses that pose and not its velocity, the jump reaches the cage as a velocity spike --- a displacement of 3.6 m in a single frame and a velocity of 5.5 m/s against a contract of 0.22 were measured --- which triggers the chain of speed rules and stops the run. Disabling loop closure removes the mechanism: the per-frame jumps go from \textbf{116 to 0} in 509 s. And the \textbf{speed ceiling of the cage can never activate} in the deployed configuration, because its threshold (0.25 m/s) is above the operating speed (0.22 m/s): what \Cref{ch:08} recorded as a coverage gap of the campaign is on hardware a rule that, as it is parameterised, cannot intervene under any reachable circumstance. This is not a failure of the rule but of the relation between its threshold and the chosen operating point, and it is documented without being corrected because re-arming it would first require a reliable curvature channel (\S\ref{sec:9.3.6}).

\subsection{The accuracy of the estimator is a property of the \emph{place}, not of the motion}
\label{sec:9.3.6}

Once the geometry was closed, the estimator kept stopping the vehicle, and the cause was located with a measurement deliberately poor in technology: four stations marked with tape on the floor, the vehicle pushed by hand with a chassis pointer following the painted line, camera alone,\sidenote{No policy, no cage, no odometry.} four laps and four probes with the vehicle stopped, about 11,500 frames. The acceptance criterion had been fixed \textbf{before} measuring, on a geometric identity that owes nothing to odometry: on a closed circuit the integrated curvature per lap equals $2\pi$.

It fails by a factor of two --- the integration gives \textbf{1.97 / 2.01 / 2.25 / 2.37} against an admissible band of 0.75--1.35 --- and that is the first measurement of the curvature channel that does not depend on the vehicle or on its state estimation. But the result that reorders the work is another one: \textbf{the estimator does not degrade from driving, it degrades from where it is}. At the start of the straight it pairs \textbf{96.7\,\%} of the frames and is wrong by \textbf{7.2 mm}; on the intermediate sections it pairs between 37.9\,\% and 60.2\,\%; and with the vehicle \textbf{stopped} at two of those points and the true lateral error at zero, one probe pairs \textbf{0.0\,\%} of its 273 frames and another pairs \textbf{100\,\%} with a bias of \textbf{$-$39.7 mm and a standard deviation of 3.1 mm}. That is: \textbf{confidently wrong}, and therefore invisible to any dispersion criterion.

Two methodological consequences, and both affect what was written earlier. The first one is that the start of the straight --- the point where the field of view calibration, the nine-point measurement and all the pre-deployment checks were taken --- turns out to be \textbf{the best point of the circuit for the estimator}: the content of those measurements stands, their \textbf{generalisation to the layout is withdrawn}. The second one is about the mechanism: the defect is not in choosing badly between candidate line pairs, but in \textbf{generating} the candidates --- edges of the same stripe, adjacent markings, or a single visible line --- so that the surviving pair exhibits a plausible width with its midpoint displaced. A temporal continuity criterion over the chosen pair would not fix it, because the wrong pair is stable to 3.1 mm.

\subsection{The three cage blockages, measured}
\label{sec:9.3.7}

The claim of \S\ref{sec:9.3.5} --- what stops the vehicle is not the policy --- was put to the test in three consecutive runs on the real circuit, all of them in \mon, varying only the parameters of the reset path. None of them managed to keep the vehicle moving, and all three fail for reasons that are of \textbf{measurement} and not of driving.

The first one shows that \textbf{the condition of one second of healthy perception is not satisfiable while moving}: against nine resets issued, the log contains 623 denied, of which 48\,\% are the wait itself never completing because the health of the estimator flickers faster than one second. That threshold comes from a hazard analysis argument against oscillation at the boundary of the trigger and \textbf{had never been exercised with the vehicle moving}. The second one tests the documented escape --- removing the lane boundary rule from the blocking set --- and discards it: the budget of thirty resets is exhausted in about one minute, each one relatching immediately, with the vehicle stopped and the estimator reading a lateral error of $-$296 mm. A denied reset becomes a wasted reset; it is not an escape. The third one shortens the wait and discovers the next blocking element: over the 1,066 cycles in which it acts, the \textbf{heading rule triggers with the vehicle 20 mm from the lane centre} and a heading standard deviation of \qdeg{19.1} against a threshold of \qdeg{25}. The same configuration, measured at the start of the straight, gave \qdeg{5.3}.

That last comparison is the one that matters, because it is \textbf{the fourth independent confirmation of \S\ref{sec:9.3.6} and the first one in the heading channel with the vehicle driving}: the heading degrades away from the good point in the same way as the offset and the curvature. The three blockages are not a policy that cannot drive; they are the stopping rule triggering on a measurement that fails where \S\ref{sec:9.3.6} said it fails, and they are the reason why the remaining work is ordered on the estimator before the policy.

\subsection{Pending experimental design}
\label{sec:9.3.8}

The physical subset of the library --- nominal, nominal with curvature and heading limit, in both operating modes --- is still not executed under protocol, and \textbf{four conditions separate it from being executable}. Three were identified before driving and are of instrumentation and of contract: a reset path for the emergency stop, per-run provenance logging\sidenote{Already implemented.} and a decision about which perception contract to use, because the heading setting with which the vehicle \emph{can} drive is not the one under which the campaigns were scored. They are enumerated with their criteria in \S\ref{sec:12.4} (T2), which is where the work hands them over.

What should be declared here is which of the four dominates, because it is a result of this step and not an inherited task: \textbf{the fourth one was added by the hardware}, and it is to \textbf{widen the lane estimator} before restricting the policy. Its accuracy depends on the point of the circuit (\S\ref{sec:9.3.6}) and its failure is what actually blocks the vehicle (\S\ref{sec:9.3.7}); as long as that condition is not met, a scored run would measure the envelope over an input that is known to be defective in regions that the vehicle legitimately visits. One single parameter of the operational domain is closed at this step and only at it --- the lateral acceleration envelope, unmeasurable in simulation by construction --- and it remains open.

\section{The gap table \emph{(preliminary physical column)}}
\label{sec:9.4}

The table now has a physical column, and its first reading is not a row but a heading: \textbf{the policy that is deployed is not the one that produced the simulation verdict}. That substitution is itself the result of the step (\S\ref{sec:9.3.3}), and it means that the physical column and the simulation ones \textbf{are not a like-for-like contrast}: they describe different policies, in different modes and under different perception contracts. They are presented together because the comparison is still informative, and separated in their heading because treating them as a clean difference would be exactly the claim without evidence that the framework exists to prevent.

Three readings. \textbf{The row that concentrated the risk no longer concentrates it.} \Cref{ch:08} identified the intervention rate of the limiter as the first gap to watch, with the argument that what transfers is not the policy alone but the pair formed by the policy and the limiter, and that the physical actuator does not implement that limit. With the checkpoint chosen by independence from the cage, the two figures differ by \textbf{four tenths of a percentage point}. The risk was well identified and its mitigation --- selecting by independence and not by reward --- worked.

\textbf{The lateral error doubles, and this was to be expected.} 18.7 mm against 8.6 mm in simulation, with a loop running at 8.68 Hz instead of the 10 Hz for which the policy was trained, and with an estimator whose accuracy depends on the point of the circuit (\S\ref{sec:9.3.6}). It stays comfortably inside the cage threshold, and the figure to watch is the maximum of 98.7 mm, not the median. What \textbf{cannot} be claimed is that this maximum corresponds to a larger real value because of an under-reading by the estimator: on the rectified path the scale is practically unity (\S\ref{sec:9.3.2}). The bias that does persist is the place-dependent one, which can go in either direction and which, without true-position metrology, cannot be applied row by row.

\textbf{The loop rate is still open, but it is no longer the dominant gap.} It is the only parameter of the deployment contract that the hardware does not reach; its cause is located --- not the camera, which sustains 9.84 Hz, but the inference time of the network --- and it has been narrowed from 7.3 to 9.6 Hz without being closed.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:9.5}

The chapter leaves the state of the gap declared precisely, and in a different state from the one it had. \textbf{The first step is measured and its result is still a useful negative diagnosis}: checkpoints do not transfer between simulators, and the coupling between perception, calibration and dynamics is stronger than the modular architecture would suggest. \textbf{The second step closes with its bring-up executed and its results campaign not executed}, and it is better to state it that way and not as a measurement in progress. Even so, the bring-up produced five things that simulation could not produce, and four of them do not depend on the campaign being run.

It produces a \textbf{falsified assumption}: the field of view that simulation had inherited from a configuration default was wrong, it was in the path of a safety rule, and no amount of additional simulation would have revealed it. It produces a \textbf{negative result about the validated policy}: it does not transfer, and the cause --- the handedness of the training circuit memorised as a steering bias --- is a property of the training distribution, not of the hardware. It produces a \textbf{positive result about its replacement}, and with it the answer to a transfer risk declared in advance. It produces the \textbf{localisation of a failure mode that simulation has no way of exhibiting}: the accuracy of the lane estimator is a property of the \emph{place} on the circuit and not of the action of the vehicle, measured with true-position metrology and confirmed afterwards, independently, in the heading channel with the vehicle driving. And it produces a class of finding that only appears with a vehicle in front of you: rules that are correct in their specification but whose operational behaviour is not defined, thresholds that cannot activate in the deployed configuration, and sensors whose failure mode enters directly into the only speed input of the cage.

There is also a result about the \textbf{method} that only appears when all the gap terms are put in one table, and that this work can state because it wrote its list of gaps \emph{before} touching hardware and kept it unedited. That a priori list got four terms right --- appearance, provisional thresholds, computation rate and yaw gain --- and it got them right \textbf{in their type}: they are the terms that a simulator knows how to represent. Everything that actually stopped the vehicle is \textbf{absent} from that list: the handedness of the training circuit memorised as a bias, an intrinsics error that the simulator had inherited and therefore could not expose, an estimator whose accuracy depends on the place and not on the action, the absence of defined operational behaviour for a correctly specified latch, and a sensor whose failure mode enters the only speed input of the cage. \textbf{Three of those five live in the measurement chain},\sidenote{The camera intrinsics, the lane estimator and the speed sensor.} one is a property of the training distribution and one is the operational semantics of a rule. \textbf{None of the five is the control policy.} The asymmetry is the methodological finding of the chapter: \textbf{the gap terms that a team trained in simulation is able to enumerate in advance are precisely the ones that simulation knows how to model} --- appearance, timings, gains --- and the ones that stopped this vehicle are those of which simulation had no representation at all, either because it inherited the same assumption, or because the quantity does not exist in it, or because its plant is better than the real one.

Adaptation A5 is fulfilled in the first half of its requirement --- making the gap explicit and structuring its measurement --- and it \textbf{has started, without completing, the second half}: there is measurement on hardware, but of bring-up and not of campaign, so that there is neither a per-scenario gap table nor a physical verdict, and the verdict column of \Cref{ch:10} is declared not executed for that reason and not for another. \Cref{ch:10} issues the consolidated verdict with that limitation incorporated into its statement, and with one precision that it could not make before: \textbf{the limitation is no longer \enquote{there is no physical evidence} but \enquote{the physical evidence is bring-up evidence and it is not scored}}.

There is one further qualification that none of the previous ones contains and that \Cref{ch:10} takes up as part of its declaration: \textbf{the envelope has never acted on the real vehicle}. All the physical runs were made in \mon, with the safe action identical to the raw action in all the logged cycles. The central artefact of this work is verified in simulation and only \emph{observed} on hardware, and the campaign that would change that is the first line of the future work.
